# Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR)

Peste des Petits Ruminants is a highly contagious viral disease of sheep and goats (the
"small ruminants" in its name), caused by a morbillivirus closely related to the virus
behind rinderpest in cattle. It spreads mainly through close contact between animals —
via nasal and eye discharges, saliva, and faeces — so it moves quickly through flocks that
are housed, watered, or grazed together, and between flocks through animal movement and
shared markets.

Typical signs begin with a sudden high fever and a dull, depressed animal that stops
eating. This is usually followed by a watery-then-thick discharge from the nose and eyes,
crusting around the nostrils and muzzle, and painful erosions or sores inside the mouth
(on the gums, tongue, and lips) that make the animal drool and reluctant to eat. Profuse
diarrhoea and laboured breathing from pneumonia commonly follow. Young animals are most
severely affected, and death rates in a susceptible flock can be very high.

PPR is a notifiable/reportable disease (listed by WOAH, the World Organisation for Animal
Health) and is the target of an international eradication programme, so suspected cases
must be reported to animal health authorities so they can be confirmed by laboratory
testing. There is no specific antiviral cure; an effective vaccine exists, and management
otherwise centres on movement restriction, biosecurity, and supportive veterinary care for
secondary infections. Its signs overlap with several other conditions, so lab confirmation
matters.

## Precautions

- Separate the affected animal from the rest of the flock immediately and keep it away from shared water, feed, and grazing.
- Restrict movement of sheep and goats, people, and equipment on and off the farm until the situation is assessed.
- Do not buy, sell, or move animals through markets while the case is unconfirmed.

## Next steps

- Contact your veterinarian or local animal health authority immediately — this is a reportable disease and must not be handled without official involvement.
- Do not wait for symptoms to worsen before seeking help; early reporting limits spread through the flock.
- Ask about PPR vaccination for the rest of the flock, and follow any quarantine or biosecurity instructions given by animal health authorities.
